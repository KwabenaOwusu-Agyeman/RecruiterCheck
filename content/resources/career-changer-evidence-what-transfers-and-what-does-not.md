Changing careers does not carry the old job title across. What transfers is a skill you can point to a specific instance of, one that the new posting also asks for. Handling a difficult situation, keeping a process running, working out where something went wrong, or coordinating other people can each transfer if the CV describes a specific case, not the old job title. The title itself does not transfer, and years spent in the old field are not evidence on their own.

This piece covers how to find the transferable skill inside an old job, what usually does not transfer by itself, how new study sits beside that experience, and a rewritten example.

## The title does not transfer, the skill behind it can

A recruiter reading a career changer's CV has no use for the old job title. It tells them what field you were in, not what you can do in this one. What they can use is a skill, shown once, doing something the new posting also needs.

This is the same standard [evidence against keywords](/resources/evidence-against-keywords-what-recruiters-actually-count) sets out for any CV: a word or a title is a claim, and a specific instance of doing the thing is what a reader can weigh.

## Finding the transferable skill inside the old job

Start from the posting, not the old job. Pick one thing it asks for, then look back through the old work for a moment where you did something close to it, in any field.

The method is two steps. First, name the skill in plain terms, not the language of either field: kept a process running when something broke, worked out why numbers did not add up, explained a change to people who had to live with it. Second, find one specific case, with what happened, not a restatement of the old job description. "Responsible for team scheduling" names a duty. "Rearranged a week's shifts with two days' notice after two people called in sick, without missing a delivery" names an instance a reader can picture.

## Three things that do not transfer on their own

Three things carry less weight than they might seem to. Seniority in the old field says nothing about ability in the new one, since the two are not measured the same way. Years of tenure are not evidence of a skill, only of time; what was done in that time might be evidence, and that is what has to be written down. A job title from an unrelated field tells a reader what you were called, not what you can do here.

None of this means hiding the old career. It means treating it as a source of specific instances, the same way a project or an internship is read, rather than leaning on the title to speak for itself.

## New study and the old job, together

A course, a bootcamp or a personal project alongside the old job is new evidence that speaks directly to the field you are moving into, in a way the old job's evidence cannot, and it can sit beside a transferred skill from the old career rather than replace it. A CV that shows both, the new technical work and one transferred skill from years of unrelated experience, gives a fuller picture than either alone. Whether a project without a job title in the new field counts at all is covered in full in [Do projects count without a job title?](/resources/do-projects-count-without-a-job-title).

## Reading the posting for what it actually wants

Before deciding which old skills to bring forward, read the posting for what it is actually asking, not just its job title. [How to read a job description before you apply](/resources/how-to-read-a-job-description) covers the method.

## An entry rewritten

Here is an invented CV entry, written for this article and not taken from any real CV, from a shift supervisor at a retail store applying into a data related role.

Before: "Managed stock levels and supervised staff at a retail store for several years."

After: "Noticed that a store's stock counts kept disagreeing with what the till recorded, tracked the discrepancy back to a mislabelled shelf code, and fixed the label so the count matched from then on."

The first line names a job title and a duration. The second shows one specific case of noticing a discrepancy, working out its cause and fixing it, which is closer to what a posting asking for analytical thinking wants to see, without inventing any technical work that did not happen.

## A five minute self check

Take one skill the posting you want asks for, and answer three questions about your own history.

- Can you name one specific moment from your old work where you used something close to this skill?
- Does the line describing it say what happened, not just what you were responsible for?
- Would it make sense to someone who has never worked in your old field?

If the answer to all three is yes, that entry belongs on the CV. If you can only describe a duty or a title, look for a different moment, or leave the claim out rather than stretch it.
