Your CV has the right keywords. You checked. You matched the posting almost word for word. And you still did not hear back. What is actually happening is this: passing an applicant tracking system and convincing a recruiter are two different tests, and only the second one decides. Keyword presence gets a CV found. Evidence gets it shortlisted. A CV can satisfy the first test completely and still fail the second.

That gap is where most silent rejections live. It is not that the system rejected you. It is that a person read the CV afterwards and was not convinced.

## The two tests

The first test asks whether your CV can be found and surfaced at all. The second asks whether a recruiter, once it lands in front of them, believes you can do the job. These happen in sequence, and they measure completely different things. The first test checks for the presence of words. The second test checks for proof.

You can read more about what the second test actually evaluates in [how a recruiter evaluates your CV](/how-recruiters-evaluate-a-cv), which this article builds on directly.

## What an applicant tracking system does, and does not do

An applicant tracking system is mostly a database. It stores your CV, makes it searchable, and lets a recruiter filter a large pile of applications down to a smaller one. Every employer configures that filtering differently: different required fields, different weighting, sometimes none at all beyond basic keyword search.

That is exactly why no tool, including [an ATS focused check](/ats-resume-checker), can promise you will pass. Passing depends on a configuration you cannot see, set by an employer you cannot ask. What a good check can tell you is whether the words a recruiter would expect are present. It cannot tell you what happens next, because what happens next is not mechanical anymore.

## What changes when a person reads it

Once your CV clears that first filter, the test changes completely. A recruiter is not reading your CV top to bottom, the way you might read an article. They are scanning it against one specific role, looking for a fast, credible reason to shortlist you rather than the next person in the pile.

That reason has to be visible quickly, and it has to be believable. A recruiter is not trying to catch you out. They are trying to build a shortlist they can defend, and they need evidence to point to when they do it.

## The four ways a keyword rich CV still fails

A CV can contain every word a recruiter is looking for and still fail this second test. In practice, that happens in one of four ways.

- **Presence without proof.** The keyword is there, but nothing around it shows you actually did the thing. A skill listed on its own is a claim, not evidence.
- **Evidence buried below the fold.** The proof exists somewhere in the CV, but not where a recruiter scanning quickly will see it. If it takes real effort to find, it will often not be found at all.
- **A claim with no outcome.** The line describes an activity but not a result. What changed because of what you did is usually the part that convinces someone, and it is also the part most often left out.
- **Relevance to the title rather than to the role.** The line answers "have you held a job like this" instead of "can you do what this specific role needs." Those are not the same question, and a recruiter is only asking the second one.

### Two versions of one line

Take a single line from a CV that mentions a tool by name: "Used SQL for reporting." The keyword is there. It will be found. But it proves almost nothing on its own.

Now compare it with: "Rebuilt a manual weekly report in SQL, cutting the time it took from a full day to under an hour." Same tool, same keyword, same technical claim. The difference is that the second version names a problem, an action, and an outcome, so a recruiter has something concrete to evaluate. These are illustrative examples only, not real CV lines or real results.

## How to tell which test you are failing

You can run a rough version of this diagnosis yourself. Pull up your CV and, line by line, ask three questions of each bullet that is meant to support your candidacy for a specific role: Does this show proof, or only presence? Would a recruiter scanning quickly actually see it? Does it end in an outcome, or just an activity?

If most of your CV survives those three questions and you are still not hearing back, the first test is unlikely to be your problem. If several lines fail one or more of the questions, that is where the second test is catching you, regardless of how well the CV is optimised for keyword search.

For a fuller picture of how these two tests fit into the wider evaluation, see [how the interview score works](/how-interview-score-works).

## What to change first

Do not start by adding more keywords. If your CV is already passing the first test, more keywords will not move the second one, and may make the CV harder to scan. Start instead with the two or three lines meant to carry the most weight for the role you actually want, and rewrite them to show a problem, an action, and an outcome, positioned where a recruiter scanning quickly will actually see them. That single change usually does more than any amount of additional keyword coverage.
